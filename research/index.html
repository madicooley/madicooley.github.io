TMLR · October 2026

Localized Operator Learning with Adaptive Partition-of-Unity Mixture-of-Expert Networks

Madison Cooley, Ramansh Sharma, Shandian Zhe, Robert M. Kirby, Varun Shankar

PaperarXivCode

Overview

When material properties change sharply across a domain, a single global model can miss important local details.

I led the architecture, training workflow, and experiments for HiRefPOU. Its nested partition-of-unity gates blend local DeepONet experts while preserving continuity. It reduced prediction error against global DeepONet and fixed-partition baselines on heterogeneous Darcy and reaction–diffusion benchmarks. The benefit of adding more levels varied by problem.

Plain-language explanation

Imagine trying to predict how water moves through soil made of sand and clay. The rules are the same everywhere, but water moves differently through each material. I built a model that learns which regions need their own specialists and blends their predictions where those regions overlap.

Skills in practice

  • Python. I led the training and evaluation workflow for a neural operator with learned spatial partitions.
  • Surrogate modeling. I led the design of a neural operator that predicts solutions for families of heterogeneous flow and reaction–diffusion problems.
  • Mixture-of-experts models. I designed the architecture around learned partition-of-unity weights that blend local predictions continuously.
  • Benchmarking & baseline comparisons. I compared the model with global DeepONet and fixed-partition baselines on heterogeneous Darcy and reaction–diffusion benchmarks.
  • Model interpretability. I examined the learned expert weights to see where the model specialized and how those regions related to heterogeneous structure.

SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing · August 2026

Polynomial-Augmented Neural Networks (PANNs) with Weak Orthogonality Constraints for Enhanced Function and PDE Approximation

Madison Cooley, Shandian Zhe, Robert M. Kirby, Varun Shankar

PaperarXivCode

Overview

Polynomials are good at representing smooth patterns, and neural networks can help with the parts that are harder to fit.

I helped design and evaluate a hybrid architecture with weak orthogonality constraints between its polynomial and neural components. I implemented custom C++ automatic differentiation rules for the fixed Legendre basis. In our benchmarks, PANNs improved on plain neural networks in regression and for numerical PDE solutions, and beat either component alone on tests with limited smoothness.

Plain-language explanation

Think of drawing a curve in two passes. A smooth line captures the broad shape, and a second pass fills in details that are harder to draw. Our model works in a similar way: a polynomial captures smooth patterns, and a neural network adds detail. Together they can fit some problems better than either component on its own.

Skills in practice

  • C++ and automatic differentiation. I implemented automatic differentiation rules for the fixed Legendre polynomial basis in a neural network for function and PDE approximation.
  • Polynomial approximation. I helped design a hybrid model with weak orthogonality constraints between the polynomial expansion and the neural network.
  • Regression. I helped evaluate the architecture on function regression and on numerical PDE solutions to see when the polynomial basis helped.
  • Model evaluation. I helped compare PANNs with plain neural networks on regression and PDE benchmarks, and with standalone polynomial models on functions with limited smoothness.

TMLR · December 2025

HyResPINNs: A Hybrid Residual Physics-Informed Neural Network Architecture Designed to Balance Expressiveness and Trainability

Madison Cooley, Mike Kirby, Shandian Zhe, Varun Shankar

PaperarXivCode

Overview

A model that can represent abrupt changes in a physical field can still be hard to train to find them.

I helped design and test residual blocks that learn how to blend neural and radial basis function components. Our Darcy flow and Allen–Cahn benchmarks show how the hybrid architecture can improve accuracy on problems with sharp or localized features.

Plain-language explanation

A landscape can have broad hills and sharp ridges. A model needs to capture both, and it has to be able to learn where they are. We built a model that combines flexible neural networks with functions that can focus on local features, and tested how well it learned both kinds of patterns.

Skills in practice

  • Python and PyTorch. I wrote all of the project’s code in Python using PyTorch, including the hybrid residual architecture and the training and evaluation code.
  • Radial basis functions. I helped design and test blocks that combine neural and radial basis function components for physics-informed modeling.
  • Model evaluation. I helped test the hybrid residual architecture on Darcy flow and Allen–Cahn problems, examining prediction accuracy and training behavior.

TMLR · January 2025

Fourier PINNs: From Strong Boundary Conditions to Adaptive Fourier Bases

Madison Cooley, Varun Shankar, Robert M. Kirby, Shandian Zhe

PaperarXivCode

Overview

Neural PDE solvers can smooth away rapid oscillations that matter to the solution.

I helped investigate how enforcing boundary conditions exactly affects frequency recovery, and developed and tested a physics-informed network with an adaptive Fourier basis. Across our experiments, selecting useful modes improved recovery of the high-frequency content.

Plain-language explanation

A recording can contain a slow bass line and a fast trill at the same time. A neural network may learn the slow part first and miss the fast one. We gave the model a set of possible wave patterns and a way to choose the ones it needed, so it could recover fine details in the solution.

Skills in practice

  • TensorFlow and LibTorch (C++). I built the original implementation in TensorFlow, then ported the Fourier PINN to C++ using LibTorch, the PyTorch C++ library.
  • Fourier approximation. I helped develop and test an adaptive Fourier basis that selects useful frequency modes during training.
  • Error analysis. I helped compare predicted spectra with the true solutions to see which high-frequency modes the models recovered.
  • Model interpretability. I examined which Fourier modes remained active after training to see which frequencies the model used to reconstruct the solution.

ICLR · 2024

Solving High Frequency and Multi-Scale PDEs with Gaussian Processes

Shikai Fang*, Madison Cooley*, Da Long*, Shibo Li, Robert M. Kirby, Shandian Zhe

PaperCode

Overview

Solving a physical model gets harder when its output varies at several scales at once.

I co-led this study of Gaussian process solvers that learn the dominant frequencies of a PDE solution. We paired spectral mixture kernels with Kronecker structure on grids and showed improved accuracy over neural baselines in high-frequency tests.

Plain-language explanation

Some physics problems produce solutions with both broad trends and tiny ripples. We built a solver that can learn which kinds of ripples are present. In our tests, it followed rapid oscillations that several comparison methods missed.

Skills in practice

  • Gaussian processes. I co-led a study that used spectral mixture kernels to learn the dominant frequencies in PDE solutions.
  • Benchmarking & baseline comparisons. I co-led the study that evaluated the Gaussian process solver against neural baselines on high-frequency PDE tests.

IEEE Big Data · 2022

Debiased Learning of Self-Labeled Twitter Data for User Demographic Prediction

Zhen Wang, Madison Cooley, Yang Zhang, Chao Lan, Ji Zhang

Paper

Overview

People who volunteer demographic information online can differ from the population a model will encounter.

As an undergraduate, I conceived the study, wrote the original Python implementation, and drafted the initial manuscript. I investigated whether FastText trained on self-labeled Twitter data would generalize to randomly sampled users and developed a bias correction based on kernel mean matching. The initial manuscript remained unpublished. After I moved on to graduate school, another student built on that work and wrote the final version published at IEEE Big Data.

Plain-language explanation

Suppose you train a model on people who volunteered a detail about themselves online. Those volunteers may differ from the people the model later encounters. My original question was whether a model would work as well on a random sample of users. I developed a way to adjust for the difference during training.

Skills in practice

SIAM ACDA · 2021

Parameterized algorithms for identifying gene co-expression modules via weighted clique decomposition

Madison Cooley, Casey S. Greene, Davis Issac, Milton Pividori, Blair D. Sullivan

PaperarXivCodeView the presentations

Overview

Genes can act together in overlapping groups, which makes it difficult to recover those groups from pairwise measurements.

Our team modeled those groups as weighted cliques. I developed the full fixed-parameter tractable integer-partitioning algorithm for the noise-free problem and wrote all of the project’s Python code, including the visualization scripts for the paper’s figures. We tested two algorithms on biologically inspired synthetic data.

Plain-language explanation

Genes can take part in several jobs at once, like people who belong to more than one club. We usually observe how strongly pairs of genes vary together. We studied how to work backward from those pairwise connections to identify the overlapping groups that could have produced them.

Skills in practice

  • Graph algorithms. I developed the full integer-partitioning algorithm for decomposing weighted graphs into overlapping cliques. The algorithm is fixed-parameter tractable in the number of cliques in the noise-free setting.
  • Python. I wrote all of the project’s Python code, including the implementation of the decomposition algorithms and the experiments on synthetic data.
  • Data visualization. I wrote the Python visualization scripts that produced the figures in the paper.
  • Graph algorithms
  • Python
  • Data visualization
